## How to read this document

Each block gives the **proposed wording**, **where it appears**, **which resources need it**, **market-specific
elements**, **source basis** and **severity**.

Three rules were followed throughout, per your ruling:

1. **Nothing invented.** Every number, distance, duration and emergency instruction below traces to a named
   official source, quoted or closely paraphrased. Where a figure could not be verified against an official
   source, it is marked **`VERIFY`** and left blank rather than filled with a plausible-sounding value.
2. **No generic disclaimer doing the work of real warnings.** There is a general disclaimer, but the safety
   content is topic blocks placed where the hazard is taught.
3. **Behaviour, not engineering.** The blocks tell a member what to do and when to stop and call a
   professional. They do not specify clearances, ratings, sizing or ppm thresholds — those are the domain of
   the installer, the appliance manual and the local standard, and getting them wrong in a PDF is dangerous.

### A note on severity

- **CRITICAL** — omission could contribute to death or serious injury. Must be in place before that resource
  is published in any market.
- **HIGH** — omission could contribute to property damage, illness, or an expensive mistake.
- **STANDARD** — good practice; protects the member and the business.

## BLOCK 0 — General safety disclaimer

**Severity:** STANDARD (but required on every resource)
**Placement:** footer of every resource, and on the library's resource detail page.

> **Before you start.** OffGrid056 gives general household resilience education, not professional advice. It
> is not engineering, electrical, building, plumbing, medical or emergency-services advice. Your home, climate
> and local rules differ from everyone else's. Anything involving fixed wiring, gas, structural work or an
> appliance flue must be done by a suitably qualified and licensed tradesperson in your area, and anything
> involving your health belongs with a medical professional. Where this material names a standard, agency or
> programme, check it still applies in your country before relying on it. **In an emergency, contact your
> local emergency services immediately** — see the emergency numbers on this page.

**Why not only this.** A disclaimer at the foot of a page does not stop somebody running a generator in a
garage. It protects the business; the topic blocks below protect the member. Both are needed.

## BLOCK 2 — Generator safety

**Severity:** CRITICAL
**Placement:** immediately before any instruction about choosing, siting, starting or running a generator.

> **Never run a generator indoors.** Not in the house, not in the garage, not in a basement, crawlspace or
> shed, and not on a porch — **even with the door or windows open**. Opening up does not provide enough
> ventilation to prevent lethal carbon monoxide building up.
>
> Run a portable generator **outside only, at least 20 feet (about 6 metres) from the house**, with the
> exhaust pointed away from the house and away from any other building, door, window or vent.
>
> Fit **battery-operated carbon monoxide alarms, or CO alarms with battery backup, outside your sleeping
> areas**, and test them. When buying a generator, ask for one with a **CO shut-off safety feature** that stops
> the engine automatically when CO builds up.
>
> **If a CO alarm sounds, or anyone feels dizzy, sick or weak: get everyone outside into fresh air
> immediately, then call emergency services from outside.** Do not go back in.

**Verified detail:** CPSC states portable generators must be operated "outside only, at least 20 feet away from
the house", with exhaust directed away; that opening doors or windows "will not provide enough ventilation to
prevent the buildup of lethal levels of CO"; do not operate "inside a home, garage, basement, crawlspace, shed
or on a porch"; install "battery-operated CO alarms or CO alarms with battery backup outside sleeping areas";
and ask retailers for a generator with automatic CO shut-off. CPSC reports an average of **nearly 100 deaths a
year in the US** from portable-generator CO poisoning.

**Market-specific elements:** the distance in **feet for the US, metres elsewhere** (20 ft ≈ 6 m — presented as
"at least 20 feet (about 6 metres)" so the conversion never reads as a lower figure). `VERIFY:` whether NZ,
AU and CA official guidance states a different minimum distance; if so, use each market's own figure rather
than the US one.

**Resources needing it:** OG-19, OG-20, OG-B12, OG-B14 (generator-specific), plus any resource in the energy
foundation that discusses backup power.

**Source:** [CPSC — Portable Generators and Carbon Monoxide](https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/468-WhattoKnowGenerators_2022.pdf) · [CPSC Carbon Monoxide Information Center](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Carbon-Monoxide-Information-Center)

## BLOCK 3 — Carbon monoxide

**Severity:** CRITICAL
**Placement:** any resource covering combustion — generators, solid fuel, gas, LPG, portable heaters, BBQs.

> **Carbon monoxide has no colour, no smell and no taste.** It is produced by anything that burns fuel:
> generators, gas and LPG appliances, wood burners, charcoal, petrol and diesel engines.
>
> Early symptoms look like flu without the fever — headache, dizziness, nausea, weakness, confusion. If more
> than one person in the house feels unwell at the same time, or pets are affected too, suspect CO.
>
> **Fit CO alarms** adjacent to sleeping areas and on every level of the home, and test them regularly. Buy
> alarms carrying your country's certification mark.
>
> **If the alarm sounds: get everyone out immediately and call emergency services from outside.** Do not go
> back in until the fire service says it is safe.

**Verified detail:** Ontario/Canada guidance requires a CO alarm "adjacent to each sleeping area and on every
storey of your home, including storeys that do not have sleeping areas", and instructs "get everyone out of the
home immediately and call 9-1-1 or your local emergency services number from outside the building". Alarms
must bear a Canadian certification mark (CSA, cUL, ULC, cETL). CPSC likewise recommends CO alarms outside
sleeping areas.

**Market-specific elements:** certification marks (CSA/cUL/ULC/cETL in Canada, UL in the US,
`VERIFY:` the NZ and AU equivalents); and whether CO alarms are **mandatory** — they are in Canada;
`VERIFY:` the legal position in NZ, AU and each US state before stating any requirement.

**Resources needing it:** all of Block 2's list, plus OG-17, OG-21, OG-B01, OG-B09, OG-B15 and any resource
covering indoor heating or cooking.

**Sources:** [Health Canada — Carbon monoxide: preventing exposure](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/air-quality/pollutants/carbon-monoxide/preventing-exposure.html) · [Ontario — Carbon monoxide safety](https://www.ontario.ca/page/carbon-monoxide-safety) · [CPSC Carbon Monoxide Information Center](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Carbon-Monoxide-Information-Center)

## BLOCK 4 — Indoor combustion (the "never bring it inside" rule)

**Severity:** CRITICAL
**Placement:** any resource about heating, cooking or lighting during a power cut.

> **Anything designed for outdoor use stays outdoors.** Never bring a BBQ, charcoal grill, camping stove,
> patio heater, outdoor gas burner or generator inside to cook or to keep warm — not into the house, garage
> or tent. Every one of them produces carbon monoxide, and people die doing this during power cuts and
> storms.
>
> Unflued portable gas heaters release combustion products and moisture straight into the room. If you use
> one, follow the manufacturer's instructions exactly, never run it in a bedroom or a closed room, and keep
> ventilation as directed.

**Verified detail:** CPSC's power-outage guidance consistently pairs generator CO warnings with fire and CO
risks from alternative heating and cooking during outages.

**Market-specific elements:** none in the wording. `VERIFY:` whether unflued portable gas heaters are
restricted in any target market before recommending them at all.

**Resources needing it:** OG-17, OG-21, OG-B01, OG-B09 and any "power cut" or "keeping warm" content.

**Source:** [CPSC — winter storm / power outage CO and fire guidance](https://www.cpsc.gov/Newsroom/News-Releases/2026/Winter-Storm-May-Knock-Out-Power-CPSC-Warns-of-Deadly-Carbon-Monoxide-and-Fire-Risks)

## BLOCK 5 — Solid-fuel heating

**Severity:** HIGH (CRITICAL where the flue is discussed)
**Placement:** wood burners, fireplaces, coal ranges, flue and chimney content.

> **Have the chimney or flue checked and swept at least once a year**, and before you rely on it through a
> winter. A blocked or damaged flue pushes smoke and carbon monoxide back into the room.
>
> Use a proper fireguard or screen. Keep furniture, curtains, washing and kindling well clear of the fire —
> follow the clearances in your appliance's manual and your local rules, not a guess.
>
> Let ash cool completely, then put it in a metal container with a lid, outside and away from the building.
> Ash can stay hot enough to start a fire for days.
>
> Burn only dry, untreated wood. Never burn treated timber, painted wood, plastics or rubbish: the fumes are
> toxic and they damage the flue.

**Verified detail:** Fire and Emergency New Zealand's home fire safety checklists state the fireplace should
have "a screen with a proper fireguard" and that the chimney should have been "checked and/or swept in the
last year".

**Market-specific elements:** local burner rules and clean-air regulations differ sharply (NZ regional council
rules, AU state rules, US EPA certification, Canadian provincial rules). `VERIFY:` per market before naming
any rule. Clearance distances are **deliberately not stated** — they belong to the appliance manual.

**Resources needing it:** OG-17 (Solid Fuel Heating Planner), OG-21, OG-26, OG-B01, OG-B09, OG-B12, OG-B15.

**Source:** [Fire and Emergency NZ — Urban home fire safety checklist](https://www.fireandemergency.nz/home-fire-safety/urban-home-fire-safety-checklist/) · [FENZ — Indoor fires and heaters](https://www.fireandemergency.nz/home-fire-safety/home-fire-hazards/indoor-fires-and-heaters/)

## BLOCK 6 — Gas and LPG

**Severity:** CRITICAL
**Placement:** any gas or LPG content — cooking, heating, cylinders, storage.

> **Gas work is for licensed gasfitters.** Do not install, alter or repair a fixed gas appliance or pipework
> yourself. In most of our markets it is illegal as well as dangerous.
>
> Store LPG cylinders **upright, outdoors, in the open air**, away from heat, drains and basements — never in
> a cupboard, under the house or inside a garage.
>
> **If you smell gas:** do not use switches, phones or anything that could spark. Turn the supply off if you
> can do so safely, get everyone outside, and call your gas supplier's emergency line or emergency services
> from outside.
>
> Never use an outdoor gas appliance indoors (see Block 4).

**Market-specific elements and sources — NZ and AU now verified:**

| Market | Verified position |
|---|---|
| **New Zealand** | Gasfitting is **restricted work**: it can only be done by a currently **licensed or certifying gasfitter**, and doing restricted gasfitting without a current NZ licence **is illegal**. This covers pipework, gas appliances, flues and any additions or alterations. Since 1 July 2013 all gasfitting requires certification, and the person responsible must issue a **Gas Safety Certificate**. Registration and licensing is administered by the **PGDB**. |
| **Australia** | Gasfitting requires a **licence** — in NSW under the *Gas & Electricity (Consumer Safety) Act 2017*, for any gas work regardless of value or whether it is residential or commercial. After installing a gas appliance the licensed gasfitter **must give you a compliance certificate**. Regulation is **state-based** (Building Commission NSW, the Building and Plumbing Commission in Victoria, Energy Safe Victoria). |
| US, CA | `VERIFY` — secondary markets, not required for launch. |

**Resources needing it:** the resources the audit flagged for gas, plus any cooking-without-power content.

**Sources:** [WorkSafe NZ — Getting gas work done](https://www.worksafe.govt.nz/managing-health-and-safety/consumers/safe-living-with-gas/getting-gas-work-done/) · [PGDB](https://www.pgdb.co.nz) · [NSW Fair Trading — Using a gasfitter](https://www.fairtrading.nsw.gov.au/housing-and-property/building-and-renovating/preparing-to-build-and-renovate/using-a-gasfitter) · [Energy Safe Victoria — Gasfitters](https://www.energysafe.vic.gov.au/community-safety/working-tradespeople/gasfitters)

## BLOCK 7 — Batteries, inverters and electrical safety

**Severity:** CRITICAL
**Placement:** solar, battery storage, inverters, backup power, generator connection.

> **Anything that connects to your home's fixed wiring must be installed by a licensed electrician.** That
> includes solar, battery storage, inverters, transfer switches and any way of feeding a generator into your
> house circuits.
>
> **Never plug a generator into a wall socket to power the house.** This is called backfeeding. It can
> electrocute line workers repairing the network, and it can destroy your wiring. Use appliance leads plugged
> directly into the generator, or a transfer switch installed by an electrician.
>
> Treat batteries with respect: they store a lot of energy. Do not modify, puncture or short battery packs.
> Follow the manufacturer's instructions for charging, ventilation and storage temperature. If a lithium
> battery is hot, swollen, leaking or smoking, move away from it and call emergency services — do not carry
> it through the house.

**Verified detail:** the backfeeding hazard and the requirement for a transfer switch or direct appliance
connection are standard CPSC generator guidance.

**Market-specific elements:** the licence name (electrician / registered electrician / licensed electrical
worker) and the network operator's outage line. `VERIFY:` per market.

**Resources needing it:** OG-18 (Solar Power 101 Workbook), OG-05, OG-06, OG-21, OG-26, OG-28, OG-29, OG-30,
OG-B12, OG-B13, OG-B15 — the 11 the audit flagged.

**Source:** [CPSC — Portable Generators](https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/468-WhattoKnowGenerators_2022.pdf)

## BLOCK 8 — Stored drinking water

**Severity:** HIGH
**Placement:** every water storage, rainwater and emergency supplies resource.

> **Store at least the amount your emergency service recommends, and treat it as drinking water from the
> start.** Use food-grade containers with tight lids, keep them out of sunlight, label them with the date, and
> replace stored tap water regularly.
>
> Rainwater and river water are **not** drinking water until they are treated. Roof-collected water picks up
> bird droppings, dust, lead from flashings and whatever is on the roof.
>
> If your supply is damaged, or you are told your water is unsafe, follow your water supplier's or emergency
> service's instructions. **Boiling is the most reliable household treatment**; chemical treatment and filters
> each have limits, and no household method removes chemical contamination.

| Market | Official storage guidance |
|---|---|
| New Zealand | "At least three litres of drinking water per person per day… at least nine litres per person for the three days". Ideally a week: 21 litres per person. Hot conditions or physical activity "can double the amount required". Children, nursing mothers and ill people need more. |
| United States | "At least one gallon of water per person per day for several days". "In very hot temperatures, water needs can double." Children, nursing mothers and sick people may need more. |
| **Australia** | **"Pack 10 litres of drinking water per person at a minimum"**, for a three-day emergency period, with a three-day food plan alongside it. Rural and remote households may need to stock for longer. Australian emergency guidance is **issued by the states**, so this is presented to members as their state's guidance with a link, not as a national rule. |
| Canada | `VERIFY` — secondary market, not required for launch. |

**Market-specific elements:** the quantity and the unit — **litres in NZ/AU/CA, gallons in the US**. This is
exactly the "litres" flag from the audit: it is not wording to correct, it is a market variant.

**Resources needing it:** OG-08, OG-09, OG-10, OG-26, OG-B01, OG-B08, OG-B12, OG-B13, OG-B15 — the 8 the audit
flagged, plus the water foundation generally.

**Sources:** [NZ Get Ready — Storing water](https://getready.govt.nz/prepared/household/supplies/storing-water) · [Ready.gov — Water](https://www.ready.gov/water)

## BLOCK 9 — Food safety during a power cut

**Severity:** HIGH
**Placement:** pantry, food storage and power-cut resources.

> **Keep the fridge and freezer doors shut.** Every opening costs you cold.
>
> Use a fridge thermometer and a freezer thermometer so you are judging by temperature, not by guesswork.
>
> **When in doubt, throw it out.** Never taste food to decide whether it is safe, and do not rely on smell or
> appearance — food can be unsafe without either.

**Market-specific figures — and they differ sharply, which is the point:**

| | New Zealand (MPI) | Australia (NSW Food Authority) |
|---|---|---|
| Fridge | food is usable if the power was off **less than 24 hours** | a closed fridge keeps food cold **about 4 hours**; discard perishables unrefrigerated **more than 4 hours** |
| Freezer | a **full** freezer, power off **less than 4 days** | **about 24 hours** if it stays closed |
| Thawed food | may be kept or used, but **must be kept cold** | **do not refreeze**; keep cold and eat within 24 hours |
| Judgement | check smell, look, colour, slimy texture; discard anything past its Use-By date; do not use damaged tins | **never taste** suspect food; if it does not feel cold, discard it |

**This divergence is exactly why a shared figure would be a safety bug.** Quoting NZ's "less than 24 hours" to
an Australian member contradicts their own food authority by a factor of six. The market layer keeps them
apart, and a test asserts the two values are never equal.

**Resources needing it:** OG-11, OG-12, OG-14 and the food foundation generally.

**Sources:** [MPI — Food safety in natural disasters and emergencies](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/food-business/food-safety-in-natural-disasters-and-emergencies-2) · [NSW Food Authority — Food safety in emergencies](https://www.foodauthority.nsw.gov.au/consumer/keeping-food-safe/flood-fire-power-cut-emergencies) · US/CA figures remain `VERIFY` (secondary markets).

## BLOCK 10 — Fire risk and emergency response

**Severity:** CRITICAL
**Placement:** heating, cooking, candles, fuel storage, and the 72-hour/emergency resources.

> **Working smoke alarms, on every level, tested regularly.** They are the single cheapest thing in this
> programme that saves lives.
>
> Candles and open flames are a last resort for lighting — a battery lantern or torch is safer. Never leave a
> flame unattended or within reach of children or pets.
>
> Store fuel in approved containers, outside living areas, away from heat and ignition sources, and only in
> the quantity you actually need.
>
> **Know your escape plan:** two ways out of every room where possible, a meeting point outside, and everyone
> in the household knows both. Get out, stay out, and call emergency services from outside. Never go back into
> a burning building for anything.

**Verified detail:** Fire and Emergency New Zealand's home fire safety checklists cover working smoke alarms,
fireguards, and an escape plan as the basis of home fire safety.

**Market-specific elements:** smoke alarm legal requirements differ (NZ rental rules, AU state rules, US and
CA state/provincial codes). `VERIFY:` before stating any requirement as law.

**Resources needing it:** OG-06, OG-17, OG-21, OG-B01, OG-B09 and the shelter foundation generally.

**Source:** [Fire and Emergency NZ — Home fire safety](https://www.fireandemergency.nz/home-fire-safety/) · [FENZ — Urban home fire safety checklist](https://www.fireandemergency.nz/home-fire-safety/urban-home-fire-safety-checklist/)
