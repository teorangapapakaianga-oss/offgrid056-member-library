### Section 2 — Why This Matters  (running header: *OffGrid056 | Household Spending Capacity Check* — *General · Planning*)
> A resilience improvement only helps if your household can afford it without putting essentials at risk. A water tank, a battery system or an insulation upgrade is a good step only when it fits your real situation.
>
> This worksheet helps you work out how much your household can realistically set aside, and how fast. It does not tell you what to buy, and it does not ask you to spend. Many early steps cost little or nothing.
>
> Use it with your own priorities, for example those from your Home Resilience Scorecard or your Resilience Product Wishlist. You might focus on one of Air, Water, Shelter, Food or Energy, or on more than one. You do not need to cover all five.
>
> Spending on resilience should move your household toward more independence, one practical step at a time. It should not leave you less secure than before.

**The Honesty Rule** (boxed)
> Use realistic figures. Do not count money your household needs for essentials, debt payments or unexpected costs. It is better to start small and keep going than to stretch too far and stop. A low figure is still a useful result.
>
> This worksheet is for personal planning only and is not financial advice.

### Section 3 — Step 1: Take a Financial Snapshot
> Write your own figures. Estimates are fine. If you are not sure of a figure, leave it blank and come back to it. These figures are for your own planning.

| Field | Helper text | Entry |

| 2. Essential Monthly Household Expenses | What you must pay each month: housing, food, power, water, transport, insurance, health costs and anything else you cannot do without. | writing line |
| 3. Existing Debt / Financial Commitments | Regular repayments and other commitments, such as loans, credit cards or hire purchase. | writing line |
| 4. Current Savings Available for Resilience Projects | Savings you could use for resilience. Do not include retirement savings, or money you keep for emergencies. | writing line |
| 5. Realistic Amount Available to Allocate Now | After essentials, commitments and your safety buffer, what could you put toward resilience now without strain? It can be zero. | writing line |

### Section 4 — Step 2: Work Out Your Available Capacity
> Use your snapshot to think about what is realistic. There are no right or wrong answers, and nothing here is a target. Write in your own words.

Prompts, each with two writing lines: *After essentials and commitments, is there money left in a typical month? **If it helps** (tip box): *One simple way to see what is left each month: income, minus essential expenses, minus commitments. Use your own method if you prefer.* — then: *What is left each month, using my own method* (one line) and *In my own words: what we can realistically allocate* (two lines).

### Section 5 — Step 3: Choose a Realistic Spending Position
> These are planning positions, not levels or ratings. None is better than another, and your position can change as your situation changes. Tick the one that fits your own figures today.

**My Spending Position** — *Tick one and write why*
- ☐ **Protect Essentials First** — Essentials, debts or a safety buffer need your money right now. Focus on steps that cost little or nothing, such as using what you already have, learning and planning. Come back to this when things change.
- ☐ **Small Steps** — You can set aside a little at a time. Choose small, practical improvements, and finish one before you start the next.
- ☐ **Planned Upgrades** — You can plan and save toward a specific improvement. Decide what you want, find out what it costs, and build up to it before you commit.
- ☐ **A Larger Project** — You have enough set aside, or a clear way to build it, to take on a bigger improvement. Get written quotes and a plan first, and keep your essentials protected.

*Why this position fits us right now* (two lines)
